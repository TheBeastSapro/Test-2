# The title is a promise, and the video pays it

A title does two jobs and they pull against each other. It has to earn the click, and
it has to be a promise the video keeps. Optimising only the first is why a channel can
have a good click-through rate and falling watch time at the same time: the algorithm
does not reward the click, it rewards what the click leads to. A viewer who clicks and
leaves at forty seconds has told YouTube the video is worse than one nobody clicked.

So the test is never "would this get clicked". It is **would this get clicked, and does
the video deliver exactly this**.

## Write several, then choose — never write one

One title cannot be judged. It can only be accepted, because there is nothing to
compare it against, and the mind supplies reasons for whatever is in front of it. Three
to five is enough; past that it stops being a choice and becomes a reading task, and
whoever is choosing takes the first one.

The point of writing several is not to have spares. It is that each one should reach
for the click a *different way*, so choosing between them is choosing what the video is
about. Five phrasings of the same idea is one option written five times.

## The devices

Each of these is one way to create the gap between what the viewer knows and what they
want to know. Pick one per title. Two stacked reads as trying too hard, and three is
the shape of a title nobody trusts.

* **A specific number.** Precision is the whole effect: `$246,000` is believed and
  `$250,000` is not, because the round one reads as an estimate somebody made up. Use
  the real figure, and only when the video actually establishes it.
* **A named antagonist.** A person, a company, a rule, a physical force. Conflict needs
  two sides and most drafts only name one.
* **A withheld outcome.** State what happened up to the turn, and stop. This is the
  strongest device and the easiest to abuse — if the video does not deliver the
  withheld thing early, it becomes bait and the retention curve says so.
* **A contradiction.** Two things the viewer holds that cannot both be true. The video
  then has an obvious job: resolve it.
* **A stated stake.** Say what it costs or what changes. Answers "why now" without a
  date.
* **A question the thumbnail answers.** Only when the thumbnail genuinely answers it —
  the pair is the click, not the title alone. Treat a question title with a decorative
  thumbnail as an unfinished title.

## The shape

* **Under 70 characters**, and the first 40 carry the click — that is roughly what
  survives truncation on a phone, which is where most impressions are.
* **Front-load the concrete noun.** The first three words are read; the rest are
  skimmed. A title whose subject arrives at word seven has spent its budget on setup.
* **Capitals are punctuation, not emphasis.** One word in caps, at most, and only when
  the word is the point. A title in caps throughout reads as a channel shouting at
  everyone rather than at someone.
* **No colon-stacking.** `Subject: Subtitle: Second Subtitle` is three attempts at a
  title printed together.
* **Say the thing.** A title that has to be decoded is not mysterious, it is skipped.

## Four checks before a title is chosen

Run all four. A title that fails any of them is not the best of a bad set — it is a
signal that the topic or the angle is not ready, and changing the title will not fix it.

1. **Read it at 60 characters.** Truncate it there and read what is left. If the
   remainder does not still make you want the video, the front of the title is doing
   the wrong work.
2. **Name what the video owes.** Write down, in one sentence, what a viewer is entitled
   to expect having read this. If the outline does not contain that, the title is bait.
3. **Count the promises.** A title implies things beyond its literal claim. If it
   implies five and the video delivers two, the retention drop is already scheduled.
4. **Check it is affordable.** A title promising footage, a reconstruction, or an
   on-camera confrontation that this video will not have is a title for a different
   video.

## What not to do

* **Do not lift a competitor's title and swap the nouns.** "Same structure, my topic"
  produces a title that fits their video and describes yours only approximately, and it
  is the reason a copied format usually underperforms the original. Take the *device*
  and write a new title with it.
* **Do not add a number the video does not establish.** "7 reasons" obliges seven, and
  four reasons plus three restatements is the most common way a video loses the second
  half of its audience.
* **Do not describe the video.** "A look at deep-sea cables" is a table of contents. The
  title is the reason to watch, not the summary of what happens.
* **Do not chase a title the channel cannot repeat.** A one-off that outperforms teaches
  nothing. Prefer the device that works twice.

## When a title is measured off a reference channel

If the channel is modelled on a creator, read their titles as data rather than as
inspiration: which device each one uses, how long they run, where the concrete noun
sits, how often they ask a question. Copy the *distribution* — the habit — not the
wording. A reference channel whose titles are 45–55 characters with the subject in the
first three words is telling you a rule; a reference channel with one viral title is
telling you nothing yet.
