# Reverse-Engineer a Reference Format

The output of this is a **template somebody could build a video from**, not a description of
a video. If what you produce reads like a review, you have done the wrong job.

## The mistake this exists to prevent

Guessing the format from the genre. A channel gets labelled "documentary explainer" or
"mystery reveal" because that is what the thumbnails look like, a structure is assumed from
the label, and every downstream decision inherits an assumption nobody measured.

A worked example of getting this wrong: a horror-explainer channel was read as a
documentary with one throughline and a teased reveal. It is not. It is an anthology of
eight or nine self-contained sixty-second segments, no overarching mystery, and **no cold
open at all** — it starts directly on the first subject. A hook framework was applied to a
video that has no hook, and a reveal structure to a video with nothing to reveal. Both were
confidently wrong about the one thing they were loaded to get right.

So: **measure the structure before naming it.** When the measurement contradicts the genre,
the measurement wins, and say plainly that it did.

## Step 1 — find the repeating unit

Before anything else, decide which of these the reference is. Nothing later is answerable
until this is.

**One throughline.** A single argument or story across the whole runtime. Has a real
opening hook, a middle that develops, a payoff that pays the opening back.

**An anthology.** A repeating unit — a creature, a case, a rank, an item — played N times.
The video's "structure" is the *segment's* structure, repeated. There may be no opening
hook at all, because the first unit is the opening.

**A countdown or ranking.** An anthology with an order that matters, where position is
itself information and the last unit carries the weight.

How to tell: read the transcript and look for the same shape recurring at roughly even
spacing. If a phrase pattern, a beat order or a visual card reappears every 50–90 seconds,
it is an anthology and the unit is what you must describe. Timestamps make this obvious
where prose does not — an anthology's segments are suspiciously similar in length.

## Step 2 — the segment template

For the repeating unit, write down, with timings measured off the reference:

- **The opener.** Its exact grammatical shape, not a paraphrase. A name as a sentence
  fragment is a different instrument from a question, and reproducing "a hooky opening"
  gets you neither.
- **The beats in order**, each with how long it runs and what it does. Name what a beat
  *accomplishes*, not what it contains.
- **How facts are delivered.** Folded into prose, or listed. This one detail changes a
  script's whole texture and is the most commonly missed.
- **The close, and the join.** How one unit ends and the next begins. "No transition
  phrase — the next name simply appears" is a finding, and an important one.
- **Recurring tics.** Repeated constructions, paired short sentences, a stock phrase.
  These are the fingerprints; a reproduction without them reads generically correct and
  nothing like the channel.

## Step 3 — the visual choreography, timed to the words

The half most reverse-engineering skips, and the half that makes a copy look like the
original. For each phase of the segment, record what is on screen **and what the narration
is doing when it changes**:

- What kind of plate — photograph, footage, flat card, cut-out on a plain field.
- What moves, how fast, and **what starts it**. "A slow push-in that begins exactly as the
  narrator starts listing physical attributes" is reproducible. "Ken Burns" is not.
- Text and graphic overlays: where they sit, what colour, whether they point at something.
- Cards and grids: what they show, how long they hold, whether they recur as a motif.

Write the sync explicitly. A zoom that starts on a specific sentence is a *rule*, and rules
transfer to new topics. A list of effects does not.

## Step 4 — what to reproduce, and what not to

**Lock:** the segment template, the beat order, the delivery of facts, the visual phases and
their sync to narration, the tics, the read rate, the segment length.

**Do not lock:** the subject, the specific claims, the wording. The template is the
creator's; what fills it is decided by the topic in front of you. A reproduction that
carries over their examples is not a format copy, it is a copy.

**Never carry over their sentences.** Their prose is their work. Reproduce the *shape* — the
opener's grammar, the beat order, the rhythm — in words written fresh for this topic.

## What to hand back

A template the rest of the pipeline can act on:

- the structural unit (throughline / anthology / countdown), and how many units
- the segment template with per-beat timings
- the visual phases with their narration cues
- read rate, segment length, and whether a cold open exists at all
- the tics worth carrying
- **anything the evidence could not settle**, said plainly rather than filled in

That last one is not a disclaimer. A template with an honest hole in it can be finished by
somebody who knows; a template with a guess in the hole gets built on and the guess becomes
a fact nobody checks.

## Getting the evidence

Cheapest sufficient source, in this order:

1. **Transcripts** for the script structure — they carry beat order, delivery, tics and the
   read rate, and they are cheap enough to pull for several videos.
2. **Timestamps and shot data** for pacing and segment boundaries.
3. **Watching the video** for the thing text cannot show: what is on screen and what the
   narration is doing when it changes.

Use more than one reference. A single video is an anecdote — one video's opener could be a
one-off, and the same opener across three is a rule. Prefer the videos that beat the
channel's own cohort: the median tells you what a channel usually does, the outliers tell
you what it does when it works.
