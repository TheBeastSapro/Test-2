# Editing Brief — Horror Explainer Videos

*Part 1 is the style standard and goes to every editor. Part 2 is the current per-editor watch list. Part 3 is archived first-round feedback, kept for history only.*

**Revision 2026-08-08.** This replaces the July version. Four things changed and they matter: the house style is now cinematic-integrated rather than white-canvas, the maximum hold is 4 seconds not 8, fan art is allowed under one condition, and the corner mascot is banned. If you were working from the old brief, read Part 1 again in full.

## PART 1 — The style standard

### House style: cinematic-integrated

Our channel is **M Simplified-leaning, not Ficknime-leaning**. The creature is composited into a scene. It is not a floating cutout on a white page.

- **Full-frame composited scenes are the DEFAULT.** The scene fills the whole frame. Background plate, creature layer, foreground element, icons stacked on top.
- **The white-canvas boxed treatment is the EXCEPTION**, not half the section. Use it for infographic beats only: size comparisons, numbers, measurement lines, icon explanations. Then go back to the scene.
- Rough split to aim for: about 70 to 75 percent composited scenes, 25 to 30 percent white-canvas infographic frames.
- **No corner mascot. Ever.** Neither of our reference channels pins a creature in the corner. The title lives top-center and the corners stay empty. On a cinematic scene a corner creature reads as a sticker pasted over the shot, and it fights the eye. If you have one, remove it.

### Build scenes in layers, not as finished pictures

This is the single biggest craft difference between our reference channels and a beginner edit.

A scene is one background plus layers that arrive over time. Reuse the background and change what is on it. Reuse the creature cutout and change the background. The story advances by adding layers, not by cutting to a new artwork every time.

Worked example from M Simplified's Behemoth opening: stick figures on a mountain, then the creature's head slides up from behind the peaks, then a boulder slides in as a foreground layer, then the sky goes red for the climax. One location, one staged reveal, escalating layers. They even tint the creature to match the sky so it sits in the scene instead of on top of it.

Full standalone artworks still appear, but each one has a job and lasts 2 to 4 seconds, then you are back to the layered language.

### The staged reveal

**One persistent background is correct. One persistent SHOT is the failure mode.**

A long beat in one location is built as several shots, not one long zoom:

1. Wide shot, 2 to 3 seconds
2. The creature visibly rises or slides in. It must be fast enough that the viewer SEES it move
3. Hard cut to a closer framing of the same scene
4. Back wide, with screen shake on the impact line

Cutting closer means an actual cut to a tighter crop, not a zoom.

### Motion is rule number one

- **No shot may ever be fully static.** Every held image gets at least a slow zoom or pan. Both reference channels run a constant 1.05x to 1.1x zoom on effectively every image.
- Icons, stick figures and text ALWAYS animate in. Slide in from an edge, or pop from 0 to 110 to 100 percent scale over about 0.2 seconds. Never just appear.
- Screen shake on impact beats. Glitch or brief VHS grain where the script earns it: broadcasts, sightings, found footage.

### Pacing

- Something on screen changes every **2 to 4 seconds**. Both reference channels measure at an average cut every **2.2 seconds**.
- **Never hold one unchanged frame longer than 4 seconds.** This is a hard limit. The old 8 second figure is withdrawn.
- If the background has to stay, add a layer. The biggest video in this niche updates its visuals every 1.5 to 2 seconds by adding icons even when the background persists.

### Text is always on screen

- **The creature name sits top-center on every single frame, including the cinematic ones.** On a dark full-frame scene give it a white fill with a black stroke, or a subtle dark scrim, so it stays readable.
- **No frame is ever empty or text-free.** If there is no keyword caption, the frame is not finished.
- **Keyword pops, never full-sentence subtitles.** One to four words. "DELAY", "NO WARNING", "36 TEETH", "August 24th, 2018". If a pop reads like a sentence, cut it down.
- Captions sit in the dead space of the frame. On a wide night scene that usually means the empty upper area, not a band at the bottom.
- Pops land exactly on the first syllable of the narrated word.
- **Aim for 4 or more pops per section, and hold that density through the final section.** Density that tapers in the last two sections is a retention problem and it is the most common failure we see.
- **Blank text-only cards are banned.** A red sentence alone on an empty canvas is not a shot. Keep the scene on screen and pop the text over it. No competitor does this.

### Icons

Black and white vector language: stick figures for people, red X over a silhouette for danger or death or absence, arrows to direct the eye, clocks, warning marks.

- **For a list, pop the icons in one at a time on the spoken noun.** Never assemble the whole grid and leave it sitting there. If the VO names four things, that is four separate pop-ins.
- Multiple images at once go in a clean 2-up or 3-up grid. Same size, same border.

### Humour

The scripts contain one or two dry-joke lines per section. Every joke line gets a visual gag timed to it.

**The gag must stay inside the horror register.** No licensed characters, no real identifiable people, no meme photographs, no random animal memes. A gag on a line the script did not mark as a joke is a mistake, not a bonus. If you are unsure a line is a joke, ask.

### Images and canon

- You will receive an editor-materials sheet per video with the approved images and the known traps for each creature. Work from it.
- **Fan art is allowed.** The hard rule is different and it is absolute: **the image on screen must never contradict the narration line it plays under.** If the VO says the skull has no lower jaw, the skull on screen has no lower jaw. If the VO says nobody has ever seen the whole thing, do not show the whole body.
- If you upscale or repaint an image, the anatomy must survive intact. When in doubt, send it before you use it.
- Never use identifiable real people as horror illustration, and never a child.

### Audio

- Dark ambient bed under the whole section, ducked clearly under the VO. Our references duck by about 12 dB.
- SFX synced to visual cues. Whoosh on text pops, boom or thud on reveals, glitch where the visuals call for it. **A cut without a sound is a wasted cut.**
- Reference channels run roughly 12 to 22 SFX per minute. If you are under 10, the edit sounds empty.
- Loudness -14 to -16 LUFS, true peak below -1 dB, no clipping. **Do not over-limit the master.** If the bed and the VO sit at the same level all the way through, the SFX have no headroom to punch and we will send it back.

### Delivery

- 1920x1080 minimum, 4K welcome. 30 fps. H.264 MP4.
- **Bitrate 10 to 15 Mbps at 1080p.** Dark scenes need far more data than white-canvas frames and they block up badly at a low bitrate. If the source images are themselves low resolution, find higher-res versions, because a higher export setting alone will not sharpen a soft source.

### Section and video structure

- Open on the roster grid of all the video's creatures, then punch in to the first card.
- At every section boundary, come back out to the grid and punch into the next card. That turns each section change into a small reveal instead of a hard cut.
- Sections run 62 to 74 seconds.
- Mid-roll CTA is about 11 seconds and lands after creature 3.
- The final section runs long because it absorbs the outro.

### Two things worth knowing about what actually wins

Measured against the biggest video in this niche, 2.7M views versus 400 to 600K for its competitors:

1. **It starts the first fact at 0:01.** No welcome, no channel intro, no "in today's video". Cold open, straight in.
2. **It uses 3 to 5 icons per concept where the smaller channels use one static image**, and it narrates at 160 to 180 words per minute against their 120 to 140.

Neither of those is about layout. Density and a cold open beat a prettier frame.

## PART 2 — Current per-editor watch list

### Vu Le
**Solved, do not re-flag unless it regresses:** motion, grade legibility, quiet gaps in hooks. His last full-length measured 25.1 percent average frame change per 0.5s, one 4.0s low-motion stretch, nothing over 8s. Best craft floor we have had.

**Open:**
1. **Asset register discipline.** The gag instinct reaches for whatever is funny rather than whatever is in tone. A Minion, a real-person meme photo, a cartoon chicken and a pig with a laser pointer as the final frame of the video all shipped in one cut. Gags stay in the horror register.
2. **Keyword pop density tapers in the back half.** Dense through creature 5, near zero by the finale. The last two sections need the same density as the first two.

Canon-daylight creatures such as Siren Head are correct in daylight. Do not darken them.

### Abel Mulu
**Strength:** best texture and cadence, and keyword pops are now genuinely strong. Canon numbers land.

**Open:**
1. **Canon image accuracy.** This is the unsolved one, three cuts running, and it got worse at full length: a wrong creature held 9 seconds, fan art contradicting the VO, an asset rotated 180 degrees so it read as the opposite of the creature. Before delivery, check every creature's hero images against the materials sheet, section by section.
2. **Motion regressed** from the earlier test. 12.1 percent average frame change, 72 seconds static, longest hold 9 seconds. Back to the 2 to 4 second rhythm.
3. **Export bitrate.** 0.87 Mbps shipped after an explicit instruction to raise it. Target 10 to 15 Mbps.
4. Title case on section titles. Two all-lowercase titles ran for 68 and 73 seconds.

### Both
- Real people and licensed IP are a confirmed repeat across both editors. Standing rule: no licensed characters, no real identifiable people, no meme photographs, ever.
- SFX on every cut. Pop spelling and date accuracy. Title case and font consistent across every section.
