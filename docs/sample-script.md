A container ship does not make deliveries. It runs a loop. Shanghai, Singapore, Suez, Rotterdam, and back — a circuit measured in weeks, with a berth booked at each end. And a berth is a booking. Miss it, and you do not join the back of a queue. You wait for the next gap in a schedule that was already full.

## Scene 14 · 21.9s

A container is not packaging. It is equipment, and it is supposed to circulate. Fill it in Asia, empty it in Europe, send the empty box back, fill it again. Break the circuit, and the boxes end up in the wrong hemisphere — stacked in European yards, thousands of miles from the factories waiting to load them.

## Scene 15 · 22.3s

And the fix made it worse, because the fix was rational. With the Asia run suddenly worth several times what it had been, the fastest money was in getting boxes back east and refilled. So ships left European ports carrying empty containers, past exporters who had cargo ready and could not get a box to put it in.

## Scene 18 · 21.1s

For forty years, the goal of a supply chain was to remove waste. A warehouse full of spare stock is money doing nothing. A ship with an empty week in its schedule is a ship you overpaid for. So the industry squeezed the gaps out, year after year, and every year the numbers looked better.

### Description

In March 2021 a container ship wedged itself across the Suez Canal and stopped one of the three chokepoints global trade physically has to pass through. It was cleared in six days. The disruption lasted the better part of a year.

This is why: not the cargo that was stuck, but the schedules that broke behind it, the empty containers that ended up in the wrong hemisphere, and forty years of deliberately removing the slack that would have absorbed all of it.
