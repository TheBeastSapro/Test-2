
## The Roman Marching Camp

At first light, in a German swamp, warriors were filling a Roman ditch, throwing wicker panels across it, and climbing the dirt wall behind it. On top, they could see only a handful of Romans. Behind that wall stood four whole legions, and their general had ordered every one of them to stand still and let the Germans climb.

That sounds like the worst order a general ever gave. The reason it wasn't is built into the camp.

A Roman army built a camp like this at the end of every day's march, tired or not. A ditch, a wall of piled earth, and a gate in each of its four sides.

And these men were in no state to stand still. For two days the Germans had trapped them on a wooden road across a marsh and killed them in the mud. It was AD 15, six years after the Germans had destroyed a whole Roman army, and now their general, Caecina, was trying to get his four legions home alive. That night they had no tents and no bandages, and they were so jumpy that a loose horse sent a crowd of them running for the back gate.

Outside, the German leader Arminius wanted to wait for the Romans to march out. Another chief wanted to storm the camp, and won the argument.

Inside, Caecina took the horses from his officers, starting with his own, and gave them to his bravest soldiers.

So at dawn, with the Germans packed against the wall, nobody moved. Then the trumpets went.

The Romans came out of the gates on every side at once, riders first, and hit the Germans from behind, pinned against the wall they'd just climbed. The killing went on until dusk. That evening the legions walked back inside their wall, still hungry, more wounded than the night before, and winners.

## The Anti-Elephant Wagons

From a distance it looked like a farm cart somebody had set on fire. Up close, it was Rome's answer to the elephant, and Rome built three hundred of them.

In 280 BC, King Pyrrhus, a Greek king fighting Rome in southern Italy, had sent war elephants into a Roman army that had never faced one. So the next year, at a place called Asculum, the Romans came prepared. Each wagon had upright beams carrying poles that could swing round in a moment, tipped with spikes, scythes or tridents. Some poles held hooks wrapped in rags soaked in pitch, set on fire. Archers and slingers rode on board.

Pyrrhus brought nineteen elephants. Rome brought three hundred wagons. I have to respect the overkill.

And at first it worked. The wagons rolled out, shoved the fire into the elephants' faces, and slowed them down. Then Pyrrhus's light troops slipped in, cut through the wagons' wicker screens, and slashed the legs of the oxen pulling them. The machine's weak spot turned out to be the oxen pulling it. The crews jumped off and ran. Still, a year after Rome first saw a war elephant, it had built a machine that could slow one down, and Pyrrhus had to find its weak spot to get past it.

Who won depends on who you read. A Greek historian named Dionysius describes a battle with no clear winner. Plutarch says Pyrrhus won, then looked at what it cost him and said one more win like that would ruin him. Which is where we get "a Pyrrhic victory."

## Caesar's Double-Facing Line

Caesar's soldiers looked across the plain and saw a long, packed line of infantry coming at them. Then the infantry started moving like horsemen, because most of them were: riders packed so close together, with archers on foot between them, that from a distance they looked like infantry.

It was 46 BC, in North Africa, during Rome's civil war, and Caesar had taken a force of mostly new recruits out to gather food. The man in charge of the riders was Titus Labienus. He had once been Caesar's own second in command, and he had switched sides when the war began. His horsemen, nearly ten thousand of them, swept round both ends of Caesar's line and closed in behind it, until Caesar's men were trapped inside a ring of riders who threw javelins in from every side.

Labienus rode right up to mock them as raw recruits. One of them pulled off his helmet and shouted that he was no recruit, he was a veteran of Caesar's Tenth Legion, the legion Labienus had fought beside for years in Gaul. Then he threw his javelin into Labienus's horse.

So how do you get out when the enemy is on every side?

Caesar stretched his line out sideways, longer and longer, until both ends crashed into the ring. That line now cut the ring in half, with riders on one side of it and riders on the other. Then he had every second unit along the line turn round, so half his men faced forward and half faced back. Both halves charged outward at the same time, each into its own half of the ring, and drove it off.

Later, more enemy troops arrived and Caesar had to fight again, but by sundown they had all been driven off the plain. A trap that should have killed every one of Caesar's recruits failed, because half of them turned around.

## The Siege Pigeons

Mark Antony had the town of Mutina, in northern Italy, shut in behind ditches and guards. A pigeon flew straight over all of it.

It was 43 BC, a year after Julius Caesar was stabbed to death, and trapped inside Mutina was Decimus Brutus, the less famous Brutus, who had also helped stab him. The people inside were desperate for salt. Outside, the consul Hirtius, one of the two men running Rome that year, was coming to help, and had to get word in. First he sent soldiers swimming across the river with letters scratched onto sheets of lead, tied to their arms, and floated salt down the river to the town in jars.

Then he tried birds. According to Frontinus, a Roman general who collected military tricks, he kept pigeons in the dark with no food. Then he tied letters to their necks with a hair and let them go near the walls. Desperate for light and a meal, they flew to the highest roofs in town, the way a cat comes running at the sound of the food tin. That's where Brutus picked them up. Soon he was leaving food out to train them.

By April, after about four months, Antony's army had been beaten outside the walls, and he had to retreat. He'd built a siege that worked on everything except birds.

## Ventidius and the Spy

As a small child, Publius Ventidius was carried through Rome in his mother's arms, as a prisoner in a Roman general's victory parade. About fifty years later, he got a victory parade of his own, for beating the one enemy Rome couldn't.

In between, he made his living renting mules and carts to government officials. When he became consul, someone wrote a verse calling for the priests, because a terrible omen had appeared: the man who used to groom mules was now consul.

The enemy was Parthia, whose armies were mostly horsemen. In 53 BC, at Carrhae, they caught a Roman army on open ground. Plutarch says about twenty thousand Romans were killed, among them their general, Crassus, the richest man in Rome.

In 38 BC, the Parthian crown prince, Pacorus, invaded Roman Syria again. Ventidius's legions were spread out in winter camps, and he needed time to gather them.

So he used the enemy's own spy. A local prince was secretly on Parthia's side, and Ventidius knew it. He treated him as a friend anyway. Then he told him, as if in confidence, that he was terrified the Parthians would skip their usual crossing of the Euphrates and come the long way round. He was lying. He wanted them on the long road, because he needed the time. The prince passed it on, Pacorus took the long way, and by the time he arrived, Ventidius's army was together.

When the Parthians finally crossed the Euphrates, far downstream, Ventidius let them do it without a fight and waited for them in a camp on high ground. It was Caecina's trick from the marsh again, with a hill instead of a wall. The Parthians decided that a Roman army too scared to meet them at the river must be weak, and charged up the hill at it.

The Romans rushed out and drove the horsemen back down the slope. The Parthians fought hard, but according to Cassius Dio, what beat them more than anything was the Roman slingers, who could hit them hard from a distance.

Then Pacorus was killed. A few of his men fought over his body until they were cut down beside it, and the rest of the army broke and ran. And according to Dio, Pacorus died on the same day of the year as Crassus, fifteen years later.

Mark Antony, Ventidius's own boss, took the war off him soon after. Dio says it was jealousy. Ventidius went home and got his parade anyway.

## The Wall of Nisibis

The king of Persia's plan for taking the Roman city of Nisibis was to turn the fields around it into a lake and attack the walls by boat.

Nisibis sat on Rome's eastern border, in what's now southeastern Turkey, and Shapur the Second had already failed to take it twice. In AD 350 he came back. His army built earth banks all the way round the city, then let the river in behind them. According to Julian, who would later be emperor himself, the city ended up sitting in the water like an island.

Then the Persians floated their siege machines up to the walls on boats, and the defenders kept setting boats on fire from a city wall.

In the end, part of the bank holding back the water gave way. The river poured in and carried off a stretch of the city wall about a hundred and fifty feet long.

Now Shapur had his gap. He drew up his army the Persian way, with armored cavalry and elephants. Inside, the soldiers packed the gap in a solid line. Even the people who couldn't fight went up to help hold the walls that were still standing.

The cavalry charged across the ground the flood had just left behind, and it was a bog. Stones and javelins came down from the walls, and the defenders rushed out at them. Weighed down by their armor, the riders sank deeper into the mud, and when they tried to turn round, their horses fell and dragged them down. Some of the elephants were wounded, sank into the mud, and died. Shapur called off the attack.

And then he saw what had been happening behind the line. A second wall was going up. Julian says the defenders had worked without stopping for a whole day and a night, and it already stood about six feet high. If the charge had got through the gap, it would have hit another wall.

Not long after, Shapur gave up and went home. Julian says his army had lost many thousands of men.

Thirteen years later, when Persian elephants went into battle against Rome again, the historian Ammianus says their drivers carried knives tied to their hands, so they could kill their own elephant if it panicked. They had learned that at Nisibis.

## The Statues of Hadrian's Tomb

Hadrian's tomb is still standing in Rome, a huge round stone drum by the river that's now a museum called Castel Sant'Angelo. It used to be decorated with marble statues of men and horses, and most of them were gone after one day in AD 537.

By then, the Roman Empire was run from Constantinople, in the east, and its general Belisarius had just won Rome back from the Goths. Now the Goths were back with an army, and they had the city surrounded. Hadrian's tomb had long since been turned into part of the city's defenses, and the Goths came at it with ladders.

They got in close, right under its walls, where the defenders' big bolt-throwing machines couldn't aim down at them. With ladders going up the sides, the men on top were in real trouble.

So the men on top agreed among themselves to smash the statues. They broke most of them into pieces, picked up the chunks of marble with both hands, and threw them down onto the Goths' heads. The Goths fell back, the bolt-throwers could reach them again, and the attack on the tomb was over. The Goths kept up the siege for about another year, then gave up.

If anyone would have given Belisarius the credit, it was Procopius, his own secretary. He gives it to the soldiers.

## The Tunnel at Dura

In the 1930s, archaeologists digging under the walls of a dead city in Syria found at least nineteen Roman soldiers lying in a tunnel. With them was one Persian.

The city was Dura-Europos, on the Euphrates River, a Roman frontier town with a Roman garrison. In about AD 256, the new Persian empire, the Sasanians, came for it. The Romans expected tunnels.

So the Romans buried their own street. A road ran along the inside of the western wall, lined with houses, and among them a synagogue with painted walls. The garrison filled the whole lot with earth, packed up against the wall, so the wall would hold if the Persians tunneled under it or rammed it. There's no record of what the people who lived there thought about it.

Then, at one of the towers, they heard the Persians digging toward them under the wall, and dug a tunnel of their own to meet the Persians underground and fight them there.

They didn't get the chance. The archaeologist Simon James thinks the Persians heard them coming and got ready. Where the two tunnels were about to meet, they built a fire and threw on bitumen, a kind of natural tar, and sulfur. The smoke poured down the Roman tunnel, and the soldiers in it choked to death.

The 1930s team thought the men had died fighting. James went back to their records and found the burnt bitumen and the sulfur crystals, and he thinks the one Persian may have been caught in his own side's smoke. If he's right, it's the oldest archaeological evidence of a gas attack anyone has ever found. The bodies weren't kept, so it can't be tested on them.

The Persians finished their tunnel anyway and set it on fire, and the tower and the wall above it sank into the ground. But they didn't fall, because the earth bank was holding them up. The Persians still took Dura soon after, and the city was left empty. The earth bank couldn't save the city, but it did save the street. For about seventeen hundred years, it kept that synagogue and its paintings safe in the dark, until the same dig that found the tunnel brought them back out.
